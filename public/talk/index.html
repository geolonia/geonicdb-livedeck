SLIDE 2 — Title: "Beyond the API — Why AI-Era Data Integration Needs Open Standards, Not Just Open Data"

My name is Hal Seki. I run a company called Geolonia. We build geospatial data infrastructure for cities — and for the AI agents that are starting to use it.

Here is the promise of this talk. In twenty-five minutes, an AI agent will read what this room just wrote — all of it. And it will understand it. Not because the AI is clever. Because of what happened to your post on its way to the database.

That gap — between clever AI and understood data — is what this talk is about.

ACT II · 2:30 – 6:00

The open data decade — access is solved. So why is this still hard?

SLIDE 3 — "Access: solved." Photos/logos of the open data movement's wins: portals, licenses, APIs.

Let me start with credit. Much of it belongs to people in this room.

Over the last decade, the open data movement won. Governments publish. Licenses are open. Portals exist. APIs serve data around the clock. Ten years ago, the question was: "Can I get the data at all?" Today, for most public geospatial data, the answer is yes. Access is solved. This community — the FOSS4G community — did much of that work. Nothing I say today takes that away.

But here is the uncomfortable question. If access is solved, why is open data still so hard to use? Why does every integration project still start with a human staring at column names?

SLIDE 4 — "API sprawl: the silo we rebuilt." A wall of subtly different API doc screenshots.

Here is what happened. Every portal invented its own schema. Every API invented its own field names, its own date formats, its own way to write a coordinate. Each one is documented, more or less. Each one is open, technically. And each one is a small silo with a nice license.

We did not tear down the silos. We rebuilt them with better licenses.

For a human developer, this is friction. It costs time and money. For an AI agent, it is worse — and this is the new part. Agents are becoming the main consumers of this data. Every new API is one more dialect an agent must be taught by hand. Open licenses freed the bytes. Nothing freed the meaning.

ACT III · 6:00 – 9:30

The meaning gap Pillar a

SLIDE 5 — "The four ambiguities." Four quadrants: Identity / Semantics / Units & conventions / Time.

Machine-readable is not machine-interpretable. Every dataset I have ever worked with has the same four problems.

Identity. Is "Chuo Community Center" in this file the same building as "Chūō Kōminkan" in that one? Which shelter is this row, exactly?

Semantics. A column named capacity — is that the design capacity, or the number of people inside right now? Those two numbers save lives differently.

Units and conventions. My favorite, because it is geospatial and it is real. NGSIv2 — an older standard for city data — writes coordinates as latitude, longitude. NGSI-LD and GeoJSON write longitude, latitude. Same two numbers. Same field. Two different places on Earth, depending on which spec you think you are reading.

Time. Measured when? Valid until? Updated by whom?

For decades, humans absorbed these problems quietly. An intern reads the PDF data dictionary. An engineer "just knows" that column seven is Shift-JIS. That was the hidden cost of every integration — paid in overtime, and never written down.

AI agents inherit all of the ambiguity, and none of the caution. Unlike the intern, they never say: "This looks strange. Let me ask someone."

SLIDE 6 — THE REVEAL: one real audience post from the last five minutes, shown as raw NGSI-LD JSON.
[Pick one good post from the inset — ideally one of the two seeded colleague posts if the early crop is thin. Put its raw JSON full screen.]

Now — look at what you have been making while I talked.

This is one of your posts. Someone in this room typed it on a phone, four minutes ago. This is what it became before it reached the database. Look at the hometown. Not a stray string in a comment box — a typed Property, defined by a published data model. Look at the specialty. The same. Anything outside that model was rejected — it never reached the database. There is a timestamp with a defined meaning. And there is a permanent ID, so this small note about a hometown and its specialty can be cited forever.

Here is the point: you did not fill in a schema. You answered two small questions. The data model did the agreeing for you. Two hundred strangers are producing interoperable data right now — without ever having met.

That is what a semantic layer is. Not paperwork. An agreement, enforced by the platform, so that meaning travels with the data — instead of living in a README nobody reads.

ACT IV · 9:30 – 12:30

The objection — "won't smarter AI just figure it out?" Rebuttal

SLIDE 7 — The objection, stated in its strongest form: "LLMs already parse messy data. Why bother?"

Right now, every AI engineer in this room is thinking the same thing. Let me say it out loud — in its strongest form, because it deserves that.

"Modern language models already parse messy CSVs. They guess column meanings. They read PDFs. For one-off, human-supervised analysis, this mostly works. So why build a semantic layer at all? Won't the next generation of models make it unnecessary?"

That is a serious argument. I have four answers. The first one I did not learn from a paper. I watched it happen.

Answer one: the confidence trap. A smarter model does not fail less on ambiguous data. It fails better — its mistakes look more correct. Faced with an unknown field, it never says "I don't know." It picks the most likely meaning and keeps going, fluently. Wrong answers that look right are the most expensive kind. They survive review.

[Shift register: slower, personal. The speaker now recounts a firsthand experience — building a civic mapping app with an AI coding agent, recently, in a hackathon-support context. The measured facts, exactly as recorded at the time:]
What actually happened (measured, not embellished):
  • The AI wrote a text-encoding name that does not exist — twice in a row ('shift-jis', then 'shiftjis'). Each looked perfectly plausible. Neither worked.
  • A data-conversion script ran with no errors. But unhandled CRLF line endings had silently turned the "capacity" field into null — in all 588 records. Zero out of 588 converted. The work was reported as "complete."
  • The lesson, straight from the log: "It ran" does not mean "it's right." Nothing surfaced the defect until a human inspected the output.

That is the confidence trap, live. And notice: nothing in that story improves when the model gets smarter. A smarter model invents more plausible names. It writes scripts that fail more quietly.

Answer two: pay once, not every time. If agents must guess the meaning at read time, you pay for that guess on every query, from every agent, forever. A semantic layer does the work once, at write time. And query volume is exploding — that is the whole point of the agent era. The busier your agents get, the better the one-time work looks.

SLIDE 8 — "Autonomy raises the stakes" + the history strip: robots.txt → sitemaps → schema.org → OpenAPI → function calling → llms.txt → MCP.

Answer three: autonomy removes the safety net. "AI will figure it out" was tested in a world where a human checked every answer. Agents act. They call tools. They write records. They trigger other agents. A misread unit or a flipped coordinate no longer produces a funny chat reply. It produces a wrong action — and every downstream agent treats that action as truth. The cost of misreading data goes up as AI advances. Not down.

Answer four: this has happened before, every time. Every jump in machine capability brought more machine-readable structure, not less. Smarter crawlers got robots.txt, then sitemaps, then schema.org. Smarter models got OpenAPI, then function-calling schemas, then llms.txt, then MCP. If intelligence made structure unnecessary, MCP would not exist.

"Smarter agents do not need less meaning. They consume more of it, faster, with higher stakes. Meaning is a contract — and contracts are what scale."
ACT V · 12:30 – 16:30

The build story — the bet, the cost, the gains Pillar b

SLIDE 9 — "So we bet on a standard: NGSI-LD (ETSI GS CIM 009)." One honest diagram: Entity / Property / Relationship / GeoProperty / @context.

So if meaning is a contract, which contract? We bet on NGSI-LD. In plain words: NGSI-LD is an open standard for describing the things in a city — shelters, buses, sensors, complaints — in one common format. It is published by ETSI, a European standards body. Here is one slide of it, honestly.

Everything is an entity: a thing with a stable ID. An entity has properties — its name, its capacity. It also has relationships: a shelter can point to the organization that runs it, and an agent can follow that link instead of guessing. Location and time are built into the model — remember the coordinate-order problem? Fixed here, by the standard. And a shared vocabulary ties field names to common definitions, so capacity means the same thing in the next city over.

Meaning lives in the data model, not in each API's documentation. That is the whole trick. It is what turned your one-line posts into the dataset filling that corner.

Now. This is FOSS4G, and I can hear the question forming. NGSI-LD has a well-known open-source reference broker — the server that stores and serves this data. "Why didn't you just run Orion-LD?"

Fair question. It deserves a straight answer — and an honest cost.

SLIDE 10 — "Four requirements we couldn't compromise." Stated as OUR requirements, not as anyone's failings.

We had four requirements. I state them as our requirements — not as criticism of the upstream projects. Those are good software, maintained by people I respect.

One: fully-managed and serverless. Our users are city governments. A city should not have to operate a 24/7 broker to own its data. If the first requirement is "run your own servers," most cities are excluded on day one.

Two: one engine for two protocols. Upstream, the older standard (NGSIv2) and the newer one (NGSI-LD) live in two different products. Our customers' legacy systems speak the old one. Their future speaks the new one. We needed one engine that serves both, over the same data.

Three: agents as first-class users, from the first commit. An MCP endpoint on the broker itself — MCP is the standard that lets an AI agent discover and call tools. Plus machine-readable guides: llms.txt, tools.json, and per-tenant API documentation that updates itself when the data models change. Not add-ons in front of the broker. Properties of the broker.

Four: security built in, not bolted on. One policy layer, with tokens tied to the sender — instead of a chain of extra servers, each holding one piece of the security.

We looked at those four together and made an uncomfortable decision: reimplement the standard, from scratch. That product is GeonicDB.

SLIDE 10A — "GeonicDB in one page." One diagram: write in (checked by the standard) → store → read out (API for apps / MCP for agents).

I just said the name, so let me give it one page. No more.

GeonicDB is a database service for city data. You write data in, and the standard checks every write before it is saved. Applications and AI agents read data out — through normal web APIs, and through MCP for agents. It speaks both standards I mentioned: NGSI-LD and the older NGSIv2, from one engine. It runs as a managed cloud service, so a city runs no servers. That is the whole idea.

Why does it exist? Because of those four requirements. We could not meet them by hosting the existing brokers, so we implemented the standard ourselves. And you have already seen it working: the map in that corner is GeonicDB, live. Your phones have been writing into it since minute one.

That is the product. The rest of this act is about what building it cost.

SLIDE 11 — "What it cost, and what it bought." Both columns visible at once.

Here is what that decision cost — because a keynote that hides the cost is an advertisement.

Reimplementing a standard is hard. Our conformance suite is about 3,200 test scenarios. We have 1.7 lines of test for every line of product code — there is more test than product. And after all of that, what we ship is honestly a subset of CIM 009 version 1.9.1. I say "subset," not "compliant," because this audience deserves the precise word. We also walked away from the upstream community, which means nobody upstream carries our bugs. And the standard keeps evolving under us. Keeping up is now our job, permanently.

What we got: a service a city can adopt without running servers. One engine, two protocols. Agents as first-class users instead of an afterthought. Security without extra servers. You now have both columns. You can do the math.

And one more thing, which I owe to this community in particular: open-sourcing GeonicDB is planned. I will not put a date on that today — but I am saying it from this stage, on the record.

Here is the sentence I most want you to keep from this act: we could leave the implementation because we never left the standard. That is what open standards are for. They make implementations replaceable — including ours, someday. Interoperability lives in the contract, not in any codebase. That is the difference between open source and open standards. In the long run, you want both.

ACT VI · 16:30 – 18:00

The payoff — what the agent is about to use Pillar c

SLIDE 12 — "What a semantic layer gives an agent." Four tight bullets. The inset map is visibly full by now.

Now it is time to keep my promise. For twenty minutes, that corner of the screen has been collecting your posts as validated NGSI-LD entities. In a moment, an AI agent will read them. Here is exactly what the semantic layer hands it.

Typed attributes — the agent knows which field means what, before it reads a single record. Validated writes — every post was checked against a schema before it was saved. Wrong data fails immediately, with a clear error. It never enters the database. Remember the 588 silent nulls from my story? That is the failure this design prevents. Relationships an agent can follow — a graph to walk, instead of joins to invent. And one MCP endpoint — the agent discovers the tools and the data models by itself. Nobody teaches it this API.

Enough claiming. Let's query.

ACT VIII · 23:00 – 25:00

Close — three questions for this community

SLIDE 15 — Three questions, on screen while spoken. Then the final line alone.

I want to end with homework — not for you alone, but for us as a community. These are questions no vendor should answer alone.

One. Should conformance test suites — not reference implementations — become the main artifact that open standards communities maintain? Our 3,200 scenarios were the hardest and most valuable thing we built. What if that layer were the commons?

Two. What is the NGSI-LD and OGC story for agent-to-agent data exchange? When no human is in the loop, who owns semantics?

Three. Open data policy took a decade to win. What would an "open meaning" policy for public data look like — and who should write it?

The map behind me stays up. Your posts are real entities in a real database. They will still be there, still queryable, when you walk out of this hall.

Open your data — yes, always. Then open its meaning.

Thank you.
